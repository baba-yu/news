1. Trillion-parameter open-weight MoE ships a first-party draft model by Q2 2027

   A benchmark posted Friday, July 31 put Kimi K3, at 2.8 trillion parameters, on three boxes with a 1,024-token input and a 400-token output, and came back with two verdicts pointing opposite ways. Eight NVIDIA B300 decoded fastest at 172 tokens per second single-stream, 1,568 aggregate at peak and 196 per GPU. Eight AMD MI355X came second at 118, 952 and 119. A two-node, sixteen-GPU B200 configuration came last on both counts at 90, 498 and 31 per GPU, so sixteen GPUs returned less than a sixth of the per-GPU rate of the eight that came first. On cost the order inverts: at the assumed rates of $2.50, $6.00 and $4.25 per GPU-hour, the three return roughly 48, 33 and 7 tokens per second per dollar-hour. The stated reason is capacity rather than compute — a B200 node cannot hold a model this size alongside its million-token key-value cache, so that configuration has to span two nodes, and spanning is what costs it.
   
   Two numbers, two orders, and a buyer has to say which one they are buying. That is the useful half of the result, and it is also where the result stops being a fact about silicon. The speed ordering is hardware and topology: 196 against 119 per GPU is a 1.65x gap that no price list touches. The cost ordering is 2.4x of price divided by that 1.65x of throughput, and the entire AMD margin is the difference between those two ratios — 48 against 33, about 45%. Move the B300 rate to roughly $4.10 per GPU-hour with nothing else changing and the tokens-per-dollar ordering flips back. Hold both prices instead and take 31% off the MI355X box, down to about 82 tokens per second per GPU, and it flips just the same. One axis is a measurement; the other is a measurement multiplied by a quoted rental rate, and only one of those two inputs is a property of the machine.
   
   Which matters, because of how the 119 was obtained. The AMD configuration ran SGLang with ROCm fixes and speculative decoding against RadixArk's Kimi-K3-DSpark, a draft checkpoint published under RadixArk's own namespace rather than as part of the Kimi K3 release, and distilled after the fact from a frozen K3 target served by a live SGLang engine. The post names no equivalent on either NVIDIA configuration. So the cheapest box in the comparison is the one carrying an extra artifact the base model's publisher did not ship, and the headroom that artifact has to fit inside is the 31% computed above. If the draft is worth more than 1.46x on this workload, it is not a contributor to the tokens-per-dollar verdict, it is the verdict.
   
   The rest of the open stack absorbs a new checkpoint in days. Unsloth published 2026.8.1 to PyPI today with DeepSeek-V4 0731 support, four days after the 2026.7.6 release its own changelog frames as the Kimi K3 one, and a merge later the same day raised the pins in install.sh and install.ps1 to unsloth>=2026.8.1 and unsloth-zoo>=2026.8.1 across all five install paths, so a fresh install now resolves to the new packages rather than the July ones. Loading a new checkpoint is a version bump and a pin. Serving one at the cheap end of the price sheet is not, because the draft has to be distilled from the base model after the base model exists, by whoever decides it is worth their time, and until somebody does, the cheapest configuration for that model does not exist to be bought.
   
   The prediction is that this stops being somebody else's job. By the end of Q2 2027, an open-weight MoE release at or above one trillion total parameters ships a speculative-decoding draft checkpoint from the base model's own publisher, in the same drop as the weights: a separately downloadable artifact under the publisher's namespace, with the serving-stack flag documented for it, and with a measured decode figure on named hardware stated in the release rather than left for an outside benchmark to find. The unit of release stops being weights plus a chat template, and becomes weights plus the thing that makes them cheap to run.
   
   Four outcomes do not satisfy it, and naming them is most of the prediction. A draft trained by somebody else and posted afterwards does not count, because that is the shape already on the table and repeating it is not a change. An architecture section describing a multi-token-prediction head, with no artifact a serving stack can be pointed at and no documented flag, does not count, because what is being predicted is a deployable dependency rather than a diagram. A third-party throughput write-up does not count however favourable it is, since the number has to travel with the release. And a hardware vendor's tokens-per-dollar claim does not count at all: it prices a box, and the box was never the missing part.
   
   What would make this wrong is the present shape simply holding. Open-weight giants keep landing as weights, a chat template and a licence; the drafts keep arriving weeks later from whoever wants the throughput badly enough to distil one; and the cheapest-per-dollar way to serve each new model stays something a third party assembles from parts its publisher never shipped, with the publisher content to be judged on the speed axis it does control.

   In plain language: A huge open AI model is cheapest to run only with a helper model someone else built. Model makers will start shipping that helper themselves.


2. Content-provenance controls ship beyond California on three platforms by Q2 2027

   Two AI transparency regimes started applying on the same Sunday, and the alignment was drafted rather than stumbled into. Article 50 of the EU AI Act began applying on August 2, with the Commission confirming that its AI Office, national market surveillance authorities and the European Data Protection Supervisor are now enforcing four obligations: an AI system that interacts directly with a person has to say it is an AI, synthetic image, audio, video and text has to carry machine-readable marks so it can be detected, deployers of emotion-recognition and biometric-categorisation systems have to tell the people exposed to them, and deepfakes and AI-generated text published on matters of public interest have to be labelled. The exposure is €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover for companies, and €750,000 for EU institutions, bodies and agencies. California's AI Transparency Act became operative the same day, and it got there on purpose: SB 942 as passed said the chapter would become operative on January 1, 2026, and AB 853 rewrote that clause to read August 2, 2026. From today a covered provider — a generative system above one million monthly visitors or users, publicly accessible in California — has to embed a latent disclosure in the images, video and audio it emits, carrying the provider's name, the system name and version, the time and date the content was created or altered, and a unique identifier; it has to offer an optional visible label; and it has to stand up a detection tool at no cost to the user, publicly accessible, that lets a user upload content or provide a URL and that supports an application programming interface a caller can invoke without visiting the provider's website.
   
   Read the two regimes together and the bill lands in different places. The EU subsidised its half: the Code of Practice on transparency of AI-generated content is voluntary, free to sign, ships a free EU icon set for labelling, and has now passed 180 signatories with a first list published — the cheap route to demonstrating compliance is a signature. California billed for the plumbing, and its largest invoice is not the one that arrived today. Section 22757.3.1 dates a second tranche to January 1, 2027, and what that tranche asks for is not a label. A large online platform — the statute means a public-facing social media platform, file-sharing platform, mass messaging platform or stand-alone search engine that distributes content to users who did not create or collaborate in creating it and that exceeded two million unique monthly users over the preceding twelve months, with broadband internet access and telecommunications services carved out — has to detect whether provenance data compliant with widely adopted specifications adopted by an established standards-setting body is embedded into or attached to content, make clearly and conspicuously available whether such data exists and which generative system or capture device it names, let a user inspect that data through the platform interface or download the content with the data still attached, and not knowingly strip provenance data or digital signatures. The meter behind all of it is $5,000 per violation with each day counted as a discrete violation, and the Attorney General, a city attorney or a county counsel can each bring the action, with fees and costs to a prevailing plaintiff.
   
   That is a detection obligation wearing a disclosure obligation's clothes, and the difference decides where it has to run. A notice requirement can be served to the viewer: check where the request came from, and if it came from California, render the notice. Detection cannot be served that way, because a platform does not know at ingest which of a piece of content's future viewers will be Californian. To answer the question for one Californian on demand, the platform has to have already run the check across everything it distributes, or run it at serve time for everybody, which is the same bill with worse latency. Once detection is running against the whole corpus, the only genuinely geofenceable part left is the chrome — the control that surfaces what the platform has already computed. Hiding that control buys a second code path and an answer to why provenance is legible in Sacramento and invisible in Seattle.
   
   The prediction is that the chrome ships everywhere. By the end of Q2 2027, at least three distinct large online platforms of the kind the January 1, 2027 tranche covers expose a per-item content-provenance control inside the product — reachable by a user the platform does not place in California — that reports whether provenance data is attached to a specific piece of content and names the generative system or capture device when it finds any. The platforms' own shipped products settle this and nothing else does. A first-party badge on content the platform's own generator produced does not count: that is a platform labelling its own output, which several already do, and it is the opposite of detecting data attached to something a user uploaded. A blog post, transparency report, policy page or help-centre article describing the capability does not count — the control has to be reachable on an actual item by an ordinary account. A bulk or research endpoint with no in-product surface does not count, because the statute asks for a user interface and the claim here is about what an ordinary reader can reach. And a control that appears only for users the platform geolocates in California, or only inside a California-specific settings pane, does not count — that is precisely the outcome this prediction says will not happen, and if that is what ships, this is false.
   
   The strongest argument against all of it is that this date has already moved once. The operative clause that fired today was itself a rewrite — January 1, 2026 became August 2, 2026 because a later bill said so — and the tranche carrying the most engineering is the one that has not fired yet. A legislature that moved one date can move the next, and if January 1, 2027 slips, this fails without anyone having decided anything about geography. The second argument is that the statute never named the specification: it asks for provenance data compliant with widely adopted specifications adopted by an established standards-setting body and identifies neither the specification nor the body, which leaves a platform a colourable reading under which nothing yet qualifies, it detects nothing, and a control with nothing to display is a control nobody ships. The third is that geofencing a user interface really is cheap once the detection underneath it is already paid for — one conditional — and legal departments reliably prefer the smallest surface that satisfies the statute when the meter runs at $5,000 a violation a day and three separate classes of plaintiff can start it. What cuts the other way is the date at the top of this. Article 50 is live across the single market from the same morning, and the machine-readable marks it compels are the same substrate a California provenance control reads. A platform serving both has now been told the same thing twice by two jurisdictions it cannot route around; the territory left to hide the control in is small, and nobody wants to be the company explaining why it shows you where a picture came from only where a law made it.

   In plain language: California will make big platforms show where a picture came from. They will build it once and switch it on for everyone.


3. Long-horizon single-prompt probes get a published run-to-run spread by Q1 2027

   Andrej Karpathy handed Claude Opus 5 the opening paragraph of The Lord of the Rings, a stated budget of one million tokens costing roughly $10, and a request for a three.js rendering. The model ran unattended for about two hours and returned roughly 5,500 lines of code that procedurally placed and animated polygon assets in three-dimensional coordinates. He called the result janky but fun, and said the field is starting to leave the territory where you test a model by asking it for a vector drawing of a pelican on a bicycle. The artifact itself is public and watchable in a browser, a page titled A Long-expected Party carrying media controls and a loader that reads building the shire, with no source repository, contribution path or bug-report route linked from it.
   
   The probe being left behind is worth counting before it goes. The pelican prompt has its own tag on Simon Willison's blog carrying 130 posts, an accumulation of individually trivial observations that nobody had to fund. In July 2026 it acquired a controlled study, Dylan Castillo's Are AI labs pelicanmaxxing?, which crossed eight animals with six vehicles for 48 prompts, drew three samples per prompt at temperature 1.0 across seven frontier models for 1,008 drawings in total, and scored every one of them from one to five on the animal, the vehicle and the coherence of the action, by model rather than by hand. That study existed to test whether labs had been training on the famous prompt, and its answer was no. Pelicans placed sixth of eight animals, the pelican-and-bicycle cell placed forty-second of forty-eight, and no per-lab boost on that cell survived correction for multiple comparisons.
   
   What carried a joke prompt from a single drawing to a designed experiment with a control grid and a significance correction was the cost of one observation. One person could afford 1,008 of them. The successor costs about $10 and two hours of wall clock per observation, and the wall clock is the harder currency: 1,008 samples at two hours each is eighty-four days of continuous running. Today it stands at one sample, on one model, judged by eye, and the write-ups covering it say as much, calling it a first-person report of a single long-context experiment rather than a benchmark. Publishing the scene settles half the problem, and not the half that binds here. Anyone can load the page and form a view on whether it holds together, so the judging is at least open to inspection; what nobody can inspect is the second run. Nothing a single playable page tells a reader answers whether another two-hour run from the same prompt would return a comparable scene or a pile of unplaceable geometry, and until somebody pays for that second run, every capability claim resting on this format is one draw from a distribution nobody has measured.
   
   So the prediction lands on the sampling rather than the scoring. By the end of Q1 2027, a publicly posted write-up runs one long-horizon prompt at least three times against the same frontier model, at a stated per-run budget at or above 500,000 tokens or about $5 of inference at the rate Karpathy quoted, with no human turn after the opening prompt, and publishes every resulting artifact. Three models at one run apiece does not settle it: a comparison across models still cannot separate a model's ability from a lucky draw, so the repeats have to sit on one model with the prompt held fixed. A screenshot in a thread does not settle it either, because the artifacts have to be posted, the way the 1,008 drawings and the playable scene both were. The settling material is that write-up and the artifacts it carries, and nothing else.
   
   Several things settle it the other way, and the strongest is that the finding would be a negative one. A post establishing that a format is noisy has no natural audience, where the July grid study drew one because it was defending a beloved prompt against an accusation. Money is not the obstacle, since three runs at roughly $10 each is about $30, less than the tooling these write-ups already lean on. Presentation is the obstacle: three separate 5,500-line scenes have to be hosted and compared by a reader holding no rubric for what comparable means, and that does not fold into a chart the way a one-to-five judge score does. The format may also not survive the quarter at all. The pelican lasted because it was free to fire the moment a model shipped, a two-hour probe cannot be, and what gets published in the first hours after a release is what becomes the habit. And the bar here is a stated per-run budget, so somebody who runs the same prompt three times and reports only elapsed hours has done the experiment and still leaves this one open.

   In plain language: A cheap AI test may give way to one running two hours for $10. Someone will run it three times to check it repeats.

### The EU AI Act's transparency article starts applying and the Commission opens enforcement alongside national authorities

- Article 50 of the EU AI Act starts applying today, and the European Commission marked the date by confirming that its AI Office and national authorities are now enforcing it. Four situations are covered: an AI system that interacts directly with a person has to say it is an AI, synthetic image, audio, video and text has to carry machine-readable marks so it can be detected, deployers of emotion-recognition and biometric-categorisation systems have to tell the people exposed to them, and deepfakes and AI-generated text published on matters of public interest have to be labelled. Enforcement sits with national market surveillance authorities, the AI Office and the European Data Protection Supervisor, with fines reaching €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover for companies and €750,000 for EU institutions, bodies and agencies, and proportionality for small and medium enterprises written into the framework. The scaffolding went up before the date rather than on it: the Commission adopted its Article 50 guidelines on Monday, July 20, and the accompanying Code of Practice on transparency of AI-generated content — the voluntary route to demonstrating compliance, which carries a free EU icon set for labelling — has now passed 180 signatories, with a first list published. The AI Act itself entered into force on August 1, 2024. ### California's AI Transparency Act becomes operative on the same date the EU's does, and the collision is deliberate

- California's AI Transparency Act became operative today as well, and the alignment was engineered: SB 942 as passed said the chapter would become operative on January 1, 2026, and AB 853 rewrote that clause to read August 2, 2026 — the same day Article 50 starts applying in the EU. The law binds any provider of a generative AI system with more than one million monthly visitors or users that is publicly accessible in California. Those providers must embed a latent disclosure in the images, video and audio their system produces — the provider's name, the system's name and version, the time and date the content was created or altered, and a unique identifier — offer users an optional visible label, and publish a free detection tool that supports an application programming interface a user can invoke without visiting the provider's website, which accepts a URL, returns whatever provenance data it finds, and does not retain submitted content longer than it needs to. A provider that licenses its system out has to require the same latent-disclosure capability by contract and revoke the licence within 96 hours of discovering the licensee stripped it. The penalty is $5,000 per violation with each day of non-compliance counted separately, and the California Attorney General, a city attorney or a county counsel can each bring the action, with fees and costs to a prevailing plaintiff. Two further tranches are already dated: generative AI hosting platforms and large online platforms on January 1, 2027, and capture device manufacturers on January 1, 2028.
