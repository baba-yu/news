1. HAWK enlarges its parameters rather than rebutting the attack by H1 2027

   Anthropic's Frontier Red Team published cryptanalysis on Tuesday, July 28 produced with Claude Mythos Preview, and the HAWK half of it lands on a standardisation process rather than on anything running in production. HAWK is a lattice-based digital signature scheme still listed as a third-round candidate in NIST's post-quantum standardisation process. The model surfaced a previously unexploited mathematical symmetry in the lattice structure that halves the effective key size, and the write-up carries numbers rather than adjectives. For the HAWK-256 challenge parameter set the work factor drops from roughly 2^64 to roughly 2^38, with an expected end-to-end runtime of about three hours and 42 minutes on a 96-core server. For the parameter sets that carry the NIST security-level claims, the gate-count estimates fall from 2^150 to 2^108 for HAWK-512 and from 2^288 to 2^182 for HAWK-1024. Nothing deployed is exposed by any of it, because HAWK is undeployed, and the result required substantial human verification after the model produced it. But gate counts are the quantity a parameter set is sized against in the first place. A 42-bit drop on HAWK-512 and a 106-bit drop on HAWK-1024 do not make the scheme attackable in the field; they make the published parameters support a weaker claim than the one they were selected to support.
   
   The prediction is about the repair rather than the break. Through the close of H1 2027, HAWK's designers answer the symmetry with enlarged parameter sets that restore the claimed security levels — a specification revision, re-computed gate-count tables, a re-sized HAWK-512 and HAWK-1024 — and they do not answer it with a rebuttal disputing the symmetry itself. The choice between conceding and contesting is not a matter of temperament here; it is set by what the result costs an outsider to check. A 2^38 key recovery is the rare cryptanalytic claim a third party can settle outright, because settling it means renting a 96-core machine for an afternoon rather than following a chain of arguments to its end. The Hacker News noted on July 28 that it had not located an independent reproduction of the HAWK-256 key recovery. That is a real gap, and it is the kind of gap that closes on its own, because anyone motivated to close it can afford to. A rebuttal issued into that situation has about the shelf life of one cloud invoice. Enlarging parameters, by contrast, costs key and signature size and concedes nothing about whether the symmetry was found by a person or by a model.
   
   The same publication's second result marks the boundary from the other side. An improved meet-in-the-middle attack on 7-round AES-128 — the reduced research variant, not the 10-round construction in production use — runs 200 to 800 times faster than the best previously known by removing a 256-way enumeration step, and it still needs on the order of 2^105 chosen plaintexts, so it will never be executed and can only ever be argued on paper. That result can be contested indefinitely; the HAWK one cannot. The cost line points the same way. Anthropic put the HAWK work at about a week of one researcher's time, roughly 100,000 dollars of API spend and about 60 hours inside a multi-agent environment, and the AES refinement at roughly a billion output tokens across about three days of largely autonomous work. At those prices a disputed result invites a second run rather than a correspondence, and that is precisely the condition under which a design team stops arguing and starts re-sizing.

   In plain language: A model found a shortcut that weakens a proposed digital-signature system. Its designers will enlarge the keys rather than argue the shortcut is wrong.

### Anthropic states a position on open weights for the first time in this cycle: never a ban, but chip controls, a distillation crackdown and mandatory pre-release safety testing

- Anthropic published its position on open-weights models on Monday, July 27, and the argument travelled through Tuesday, July 28. What Anthropic asks for instead is three specific things. ### A model-found attack halves the effective key size of a NIST post-quantum candidate, while a maximum-severity orchestrator zero-day and an AI-assisted kernel exploit surface in the same reporting cycle

- Anthropic's Frontier Red Team published cryptanalysis on Tuesday, July 28 produced with Claude Mythos Preview, and the interesting part is that the two results sit on opposite sides of the practicality line. Against HAWK — a lattice-based digital signature scheme still listed as a third-round candidate in NIST's post-quantum standardisation process — the model surfaced a previously unexploited mathematical symmetry in the lattice structure that halves the effective key size. For the HAWK-256 challenge parameter set that drops the work factor from roughly 2^64 to roughly 2^38, with an expected end-to-end runtime of about three hours and 42 minutes on a 96-core server; gate-count estimates for the NIST security-level parameters fall too, from 2^150 to 2^108 for HAWK-512 and from 2^288 to 2^182 for HAWK-1024. The second result is an improved meet-in-the-middle attack on 7-round AES-128 — the reduced research variant, not the 10-round standard in production use — which removes a 256-way enumeration step through an invariant the write-up calls the Möbius Bridge and runs 200 to 800 times faster than the previous best depending on how it is measured. It still needs on the order of 2^105 chosen plaintexts, so it stays theoretical. The costs are as notable as the results: Anthropic puts the HAWK work at about a week of one researcher's time and roughly 100,000 dollars of API spend, roughly 60 hours inside a multi-agent environment, and the AES refinement at roughly a billion output tokens across about three days of largely autonomous work with human input reduced to encouragement. Neither attack touches a deployed system, since HAWK is undeployed and the AES target is artificially reduced, and both required substantial human verification afterwards. The Hacker News noted in its July 28 write-up that it had not located an independent reproduction of the HAWK-256 key recovery.
