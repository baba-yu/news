The reason is the part worth holding onto: this is not a price decision.    What that produces is two bills of materials — one for the United States, one for everywhere else — and the thing to notice is that no law requires it. There is no US rule stopping a retailer selling a laptop with ChangXin memory inside it, and no rule requiring the box to say whose memory it is. The split is a private risk judgement, made by three companies about a line they expect rather than one they have been given, and it is being made in the same season Washington is deciding whether to draw that line for real.
   
   The restrictions that do exist point somewhere else. ChangXin sits on the Pentagon's roster of companies identified as Chinese military companies; on the reporting, an update published on June 8 kept it there after a February draft briefly dropped it and was withdrawn within hours. That listing reaches federal purchasing, not a consumer notebook. The export-control machinery reaches elsewhere again: since October 2022 the equipment and technology needed to make DRAM at 18-nanometre half-pitch or finer has required a licence to ship into China, which constrains what ChangXin can buy, and the company has built its roadmap without extreme-ultraviolet lithography as a consequence. Yangtze Memory, its flash-side counterpart, has been on the Commerce Department's Entity List since the end of 2022. ChangXin has not.
   
   It has come close more than once. Congress has been asking since 2023, when Beijing barred Micron from critical-infrastructure use and the chair of the House select committee on China called for curbs in response. Draft additions compiled inside the Bureau of Industry and Security have reportedly carried ChangXin at the top, alongside subsidiaries of Semiconductor Manufacturing International and Yangtze Memory. And in June, again on the reporting, the Commerce Department paused a recommendation to add it, to avoid disturbing trade negotiations with Beijing. Then ChangXin listed in Shanghai in late July, the debut surged hard enough to be a story in itself, and the question went back in front of Capitol Hill with a market capitalisation attached to it.
   
   The prediction is that the pause holds. Through the close of Q4 2027, ChangXin Memory Technologies is not on the Entity List.
   
   Two forces hold it off, and only one of them is politics. The first is that the instrument does not fit the grievance. The Entity List governs what American suppliers may ship to a named company — tools, parts, technology — and says nothing about whether HP may buy that company's output. Listing ChangXin would tighten an equipment constraint that is already tight and would not remove a single memory die from a notebook. What would do that is a purchasing rule or a tariff, which is where the congressional letters have started pointing.
   
   The second force is new, and this morning's report is what makes it new. For the first time the company under consideration supplies parts that American brands need in order to ship product. A listing during a shortage does not land on Beijing; it lands on HP, Asus and Acer, and then on the price of a laptop, at a moment when memory cost is already the thing setting device prices. The political economy inverted while nobody was watching it — the same scarcity that pulled ChangXin into Western notebooks is what makes closing the door expensive.
   
   One document settles this. The prediction is wrong if a rule published in the Federal Register amends the Entity List to add ChangXin Memory Technologies, or an entity majority-owned by it, with an effective date on or before 31 December 2027. Several things that may well happen do not count. Continued listing by the Pentagon does not count, because it is already there. A federal procurement prohibition on covered Chinese semiconductors does not count, and neither does a tariff, an outbound-investment restriction or a customs enforcement action, because none of them is the Entity List and the substance of the claim is which instrument gets used. A leaked draft, a proposed rule that never goes final, a committee letter or a hearing does not count. Adding a different Chinese memory maker does not count. Nor does an action from the Chinese side — an export-licence regime on memory, a retaliation measure — however much it would move the market.
   
   The strongest objection is that the pause was tied to a negotiation, and negotiations break; a truce that lapses in 2027 puts a rule that is already drafted back on a desk, where it then costs only a signature. The second is that this is partly a bet on the shortage lasting: if memory loosens through 2027, the cost of listing falls back to where it sat in 2024 and the argument holding it up evaporates. The third is that a company which has just raised public capital and is pushing toward high-bandwidth parts is walking into precisely the profile that gets listed.
   
   One thing will not settle it either way, and it is worth saying because it is where the reporting will go looking. Whether ChangXin memory crosses into a machine sold in the United States will not be established by any of these three companies saying so. Die-level sourcing is not published, spec sheets state a capacity and a speed grade, and the vendors have every reason to keep it that way. What identifies it will be a teardown from a named lab, or a reviewer reading the manufacturer identifier out of a shipping machine — inspection, not disclosure.

It fires when a developer, or an agent working on their behalf, opens the project, which means it lands on a workstation holding the credentials worth taking rather than in a build container designed to be thrown away. The control standing in front of it is a single decision about whether the workspace is trusted, taken once, at the moment someone is trying to start work, about a folder they cloned on purpose. It is a control most people click through, and the reason they click through is that clicking through is what the product is for. Nor does a third-party extension, and nor does a scanner learning to flag these files - which is the other and cheaper place this could land, and the honest failure mode rather than a technicality. ### A self-replicating npm worm takes over the keyv caching packages and leaves a start-up hook inside developers' agent configuration

- A credential-stealing worm swept the npm registry today after an attacker took over the GitHub account of the maintainer behind keyv, a caching library pulled roughly 127 million times a week, and used the repository's own GitHub Actions workflow to cut poisoned releases. Because the malicious files went straight to each project's main branch before a release was tagged, the compromised versions carried valid provenance signatures — the exact artefact a supply-chain auditor checks to conclude a package is clean. Aikido counted at least 868 packages across 1,381 versions by early afternoon Central European time and was still counting; the same maintainer's flat-cache, file-entry-cache, cacheable-request and the @cacheable scope went with it, so the blast radius runs past two billion installs a month. The payload is a preinstall script that fetches a compiled bundle and sweeps npm tokens, GitHub credentials of every flavour including short-lived OIDC tokens, AWS keys and instance-metadata credentials, Kubernetes service-account tokens, Vault, Stripe and Slack keys, SSH keys, Docker configs and Terraform state, encrypts them and pushes them to attacker-controlled repositories, with a fallback endpoint when that path is blocked. It then republishes itself through any publish token it finds, which is why researchers watched it move between organisations every two to seven minutes. The detail worth pausing on for anyone running coding agents: the poisoned repository also shipped a .claude/settings.json carrying a session-start hook and a .vscode/tasks.json task set to fire when the folder opens, both pointing at the same setup script — a second execution path that triggers when a developer opens the project in an editor or an agent rather than when they install it. Both require the user to trust the workspace, which is a control most people click through. Releases tied to Deliveroo, Qlik and Picsart were caught in the spread. ### An 80-billion-parameter Qwen runs on a Mac in 4.3 GB of memory by streaming its experts off the disk

- A Swift and Metal runtime called Swiftlet went up today with a claim that is easy to state and awkward to argue with: Qwen3-Next-80B, 42 gigabytes of weights on disk, generating at 4.5 to 5 tokens a second on an M5 Mac while peaking at 4.3 gigabytes of resident memory. The 35-billion-parameter Qwen3.6 runs the same way in 2.6 gigabytes at 7 to 11 tokens a second, and on an iPhone 17 in about 2.5 gigabytes at roughly one token a second. The mechanism is architectural rather than a new quantisation: these are sparse mixture-of-experts models that touch only around three billion parameters per token, so the runtime keeps the dense part — attention, embeddings, routers — permanently resident at four bits, packs the routed experts into fixed-stride containers each retrievable in a single read, and streams whichever experts the router asks for, holding the recent and frequent ones in a cache the user sizes with a command-line flag. Gated DeltaNet linear attention across three-quarters of the layers stops the key-value cache from eating the savings back. The author reports cache hit rates between 43 and 70 percent depending on how much memory is handed over and, more interestingly, throughput that barely moves as that budget changes, because the bottleneck currently sits in GPU dispatch rather than in reading from storage. That is the number to watch: it says the streaming approach has headroom left, and it reframes the memory ceiling on a phone or a laptop as a scheduling problem rather than a capacity one. Apache 2.0, eight commits, and written with a coding agent.
