1. Exposed MCP tool endpoints become a named initial-access vector by H1 2027

Noma Labs published CVE-2026-59726, which it named RufRoot, on Wednesday, July 29, at the maximum CVSS score of 10.0.    Unit 42's reconstruction, published Thursday, July 30, supplies the other end of the measurement. An operator tracked as knaithe wired DeepSeek into the Hermes Agent framework and drove it from Telegram as an autonomous offensive operator, using Anthropic's Claude Code for connectivity and proxy validation and leaving OpenAI's Codex traces in the exploit-development directories. In early May 2026 the autonomous phase attempted three flaws scored between 9.8 and 10.0 on its own and failed on all three, and the recorded reason is the same each time: an authentication default that happened to be on. Against Langflow's CVE-2026-33017 the agent's own working note says the exploit needs a public flow identifier but auto_login is disabled, so it is stuck. Against the n8n pair, CVE-2026-21858 at 10.0 and CVE-2025-68613 at 9.9, it records that the exploit needs an unauthenticated form with file upload and that these forms have authentication enabled. Every confirmed compromise in the campaign came from the operator's own hands, through Citrix NetScaler's CVE-2026-3055 and Marimo Notebook's CVE-2026-39987, and across more than 460 targeted systems the whole effort produced three. Reconnaissance was never the constraint: a FOFA query returned 647,017 n8n instances worldwide and 25,209 inside China, of which the agent sampled roughly 100 and probed about 40 unique addresses. What it could not do was get past a login.
   
   Put the two ends together and the prediction follows. An attack loop that enumerates the internet cheaply and cannot defeat a password will convert on exactly one kind of surface: a high-privilege tool broker that answers unauthenticated HTTP. Ruflo is not the only project shipping that surface. SiYuan's CVE-2026-66012, also scored 10.0, left 31 Model Context Protocol tools reachable through an unguarded POST /mcp whenever its publish server ran in anonymous mode, and the same shape turned up in nginx-ui in March 2026, where command-execution requests were not authenticated at all. What has not happened is the crossing. Every case in this class so far is a research disclosure — Noma Labs, OX Security, JFrog, Invariant Labs — and the confirmed real-world incidents involving this protocol are a different shape entirely: hostile packages a victim installs, such as the counterfeit Oura server that carried StealC, and poisoned tool descriptions that steer a legitimate server. None of these identifiers appears in the known-exploited-vulnerabilities catalogue the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency maintains.
   
   By the end of H1 2027, a named threat-intelligence publisher — Unit 42, Google Threat Intelligence, Microsoft, CrowdStrike, Anthropic, or a national computer emergency response team — documents an intrusion in which the confirmed initial access was an internet-reachable, unauthenticated Model Context Protocol tool endpoint on a real deployment. Research advisories and proof-of-concept write-ups do not count, honeypot telemetry on its own does not count, and neither does the supply-chain shape where a victim installs a hostile server or a prompt injection redirects a legitimate one. The claim is narrower than that: a broker left listening on a network, found by someone who did not own it, and driven through its own tool interface. An entry for one of these identifiers in that known-exploited catalogue would settle it just as firmly.
   
   What would falsify this is not a shortage of exposed brokers. Three things could keep the crossing off the record. Maintainers are closing the surface quickly and in the right place — Ruflo went from disclosure to a loopback default inside 24 hours, and a fail-closed default that refuses public binding without authentication removes the class from shipped configurations rather than leaving it to each operator. Attribution on this vector is genuinely hard, because a tool invocation arriving over the broker's own protocol looks like the traffic the broker exists to carry, and a responder can record the outcome without ever naming the entry point. And the cheaper path stays open: an operator who wants scale still has 647,017 n8n instances to work through and no reason to touch an agent harness at all.

   In plain language: Researchers keep finding AI agent servers left open with no password. Soon a real break-in report will name one as the way in.
